Notes on benchmarks & data construction

Long Video Understanding vs. Camera Roll VQA

Two ways of asking a machine to remember what it saw — one continuous stream, one scattered archive.
By Thao Nguyen · draft, June 2026
Work in progress · this post grows in 3 steps

Both long video understanding and camera roll VQA ask a model to answer questions over a large stream of visual content that no longer fits in a single context window. But the two settings differ in almost everything that matters for how we build the data: where the visuals come from, how they are ordered in time, how personal they are, and what kind of question a human would actually want to ask. This post maps out those differences.

We are writing it in three steps:

The two paradigms at a glance

Before the long lists, here is the contrast in one table. (We will revisit and sharpen these claims with data in Steps 2 and 3.)

Dimension Long Video Understanding Camera Roll VQA
Input unit One temporally continuous video (a stream of frames). A set of discrete personal photos, sparsely sampled across life.
Temporal structure Dense & ordered: seconds→minutes→hours within one event/story. Fragmented: thousands of photos over 2–6 years, many disjoint events.
Scale Minutes to ~2 hours of footage per item. Hundreds–thousands of images per user (often 1–10M tokens if naively fed).
Source YouTube, movies/TV, egocentric recordings, surveillance. Real users' phone camera rolls (+ curated public photo streams).
Personalization Low — content & answers are largely shared across examples. High — ~90% of gold answers are unique to a single user.
Typical query MCQ on plot, action, temporal order, fine-grained perception. First-person semantic & episodic questions ("What did I eat after…?").
Core difficulty Long-context reasoning over a continuous stream. Navigate / retrieve over huge personal memory + personalized grounding.

Step 2 · Question / task analysis

To move past hand-waving, we downloaded the question text only (no videos) from seven long-video benchmarks and lined them up against camroll. Crucially, camroll is not one benchmark — it is a suite of five sub-benchmarks (ATM-Bench, DIS-Bench, Persona-MME, In-house, photo-bench), each with its own question style. We treat each as a separate set below. In total that is ~102k questions: Video-MME (2,700), LongVideoBench (6,678), LVBench (1,549), EgoLifeQA (500*), VideoOdyssey (2,680), MovieQA (14,944), EPIC-KITCHENS narrations (67,217), and the five camroll subsets (5,857 combined). We then measured answer format, the question-word mix, and two hallmarks of personal memory: first-person framing and episodic / personal-time references.

The headline. Almost every long-video benchmark is third-person multiple-choice over a single video. Every camroll subset — and the egocentric outlier EgoLifeQA — instead makes the user the subject, and none of them use MCQ. But the camroll subsets are far from uniform: they span semantic self-facts (In-house, 96% first-person), episodic recall (ATM-Bench), conversational persona (Persona-MME), photo retrieval (DIS-Bench), and terse tag-style prompts (photo-bench).

Takeaway for Step 3. The split is not just “harder questions” — it is a different question-design philosophy. Long-video benchmarks optimize for clean, gradeable MCQs about a shared video; camera-roll VQA wants open, first-person, time-anchored answers that only the owner could verify. And notice that camroll itself is not one task but five — episodic recall, retrieval, conversational persona, semantic self-facts, and tag prompts. The personal-memory space hasn’t converged on a single format, which is exactly what makes constructing these benchmarks hard — the subject of Step 3.

Method & caveats. Questions pulled from each benchmark's released annotations (HuggingFace / GitHub), text only. * EgoLifeQA is the public JAKE subset (500 of ~3,000 Q). MovieQA counts include train+val+test (test answers are gated). EPIC-KITCHENS is action narration, included as the egocentric “ground-truth events” contrast, not a QA set. First-person / episodic are measured by keyword matching, so treat them as lower bounds.

Step 3 · Core challenges & construction differences (later)

Open questions to explore once Step 2 gives us evidence:

  • Why do long-video benchmarks lean on MCQ while personal memory wants open-ended, first-person answers?
  • Who writes the questions? LLM-synthesized vs. human-posed vs. self-annotated — and what each choice trades off.
  • Personalization & the long tail: shared answer vocabularies (video) vs. user-unique answers (~90% in camroll).
  • Continuous stream vs. scattered archive: how the input shape changes what "long-context" even means.
  • Privacy & data access constraints unique to real personal data.
